Testosterone Therapy & Erythrocytosis — When the Blood Grows Too Thick

Published July 2026  ·  Science in this area continues to evolve — this lecture reflects research available at the time of writing. Consult a qualified clinician before making health decisions.

Testosterone Replacement Therapy Is Among the Most Common Interventions in Men Over Fifty, and It Reliably Does Something Useful and Something Hazardous at the Same Time. It Restores Energy, Muscle, Libido, and Mood — and It Stimulates the Bone Marrow to Make More Red Blood Cells. In Most Men This Second Effect Is Modest. In Some, It Pushes the Hematocrit — the Fraction of Blood Volume Occupied by Red Cells — Past the Point Where the Blood Thickens Enough to Raise the Risk of Clotting. This Is "Erythrocytosis," the Most Common Dose-Limiting Side Effect of Testosterone Therapy. It Is Predictable, Monitorable, and Manageable — but Only If It Is Watched For. This Lecture Explains the Mechanism, the Thresholds That Trigger Action, the Genuine Debate Over How Best to Correct It, and the Monitoring That Makes Testosterone Therapy Safe Over the Long Run

I. The 'Why' — The Predictable Price of a Popular Therapy

Testosterone Does Not Only Build Muscle — It Was, Historically, a Treatment for Anemia, Because It Powerfully Stimulates Red Cell Production

Before erythropoietin (EPO) could be manufactured, testosterone and its derivatives were used clinically to treat certain anemias — precisely because androgens are potent stimulators of red blood cell production. This historical fact is the key to understanding the modern side effect: the erythrocytosis seen with testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) is not an idiosyncratic quirk but a direct, expected extension of one of testosterone's core physiological actions. When men over fifty receive TRT for the symptoms of low testosterone — fatigue, low libido, loss of muscle and bone, depressed mood — they receive the muscle-and-mood benefits and the red-cell-stimulating effect as an inseparable package. In most men the red-cell effect is mild and clinically irrelevant. In a meaningful minority, it drives the hematocrit (the percentage of blood volume occupied by red blood cells; normal for men is roughly 41–50%) above the range where blood viscosity begins to matter.

The theoretical and clinical gap this addresses is a real one: erythrocytosis is the single most common dose-limiting adverse effect of testosterone therapy, and it is the reason TRT requires ongoing blood monitoring rather than a "set it and forget it" prescription. The concern is not the red cells themselves but what they do to the blood's physical properties. Blood is a suspension of cells in plasma, and its viscosity (resistance to flow — "thickness") rises sharply, non-linearly, as the hematocrit climbs. Thicker blood flows less easily, imposes more work on the heart, and — the central worry — is associated with an increased tendency to form clots (thrombosis), which can manifest as deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, stroke, or heart attack. The purpose of monitoring is to catch the rising hematocrit before it reaches the range where this thrombotic risk becomes clinically meaningful, and to intervene early with the well-established management levers. Erythrocytosis is, in other words, a manageable side effect — its danger lies almost entirely in going unmonitored.

II. Foundational Bridge — Blood Is a Fluid in a Pipe Network, and Thickness Has a Steep Cost

Pumping Honey Through Pipes Is Harder Than Pumping Water — and the Difficulty Rises Faster Than the Thickness

Consider the circulatory system as what it physically is: a pump (the heart) driving a fluid (blood) through a branching network of pipes (the vasculature). The engineering of fluid flow through pipes is governed by well-understood physics, and one relationship is central here. The resistance to flow through a pipe depends on the fluid's viscosity — and crucially, the relationship between hematocrit and blood viscosity is non-linear. As the fraction of red cells rises, viscosity does not rise proportionally; it rises with accelerating steepness. Going from a hematocrit of 45% to 50% adds modest viscosity; going from 50% to 55% adds considerably more; and above roughly 55–60%, viscosity climbs very steeply. This is why the thresholds for concern are not arbitrary lines but reflect a genuine inflection in the physics: below a certain hematocrit the blood flows easily, and above it the fluid becomes meaningfully harder to move.

Two consequences follow from this fluid-dynamic reality, and both map onto the clinical risks. First, the pump works harder: a heart driving thicker blood against higher resistance expends more energy for the same output — a chronic increase in cardiac workload. Second, and more dangerously, flow slows in the smallest and lowest-pressure vessels — the venules and capillaries where blood already moves slowly. In fluid terms, a more viscous fluid is more prone to sluggish, stagnant flow in the low-velocity regions of a network, and stagnant blood is the setting in which clots form (one of the three classical factors in Virchow's triad for thrombosis is stasis — slow or turbulent flow). The engineering analogy makes the danger intuitive: thickening the working fluid in any pump-and-pipe network raises the load on the pump and creates zones of sluggish flow where the fluid can gel or clog. The circulatory system is exactly such a network, and testosterone-induced erythrocytosis is exactly the act of thickening its working fluid. The management goal, in these terms, is to keep the fluid thin enough that the pump is not overworked and no region falls into dangerous stasis.

Figure 1 — Mechanism, Thresholds, and the Management Cascade
Testosterone raises hematocrit through three parallel mechanisms: an EPO rise that resets the EPO/hematocrit setpoint rightward (the same EPO level now maintains a higher hematocrit), dose-dependent hepcidin suppression that increases iron availability for red cell production, and direct marrow stimulation. Rising hematocrit raises blood viscosity non-linearly — steeply above ~54% — increasing thrombotic risk via stasis. Guideline thresholds: within range below 50%; a relative contraindication to starting above 50% at baseline; and a threshold to hold or reduce above 54%. The management cascade runs from dose reduction and formulation change to phlebotomy (debated) and, if uncontrollable, discontinuation. Contributing amplifiers — dose, injectable formulation, untreated sleep apnea, dehydration, smoking, altitude — should be addressed first.
III. The Core Mechanism — Three Pathways and Their Amplifiers
The EPO Setpoint Shift

A New, Higher Equilibrium — Not a Transient Bump

The central mechanism is elegant and, in control-theory terms, a setpoint reset. Testosterone triggers an initial rise in erythropoietin (EPO) — the hormone that signals the bone marrow to produce red cells. Over months, EPO levels drift back down toward baseline, but the hematocrit does not return to baseline. Instead, a randomized-controlled trial demonstrated that testosterone establishes a new EPO/hemoglobin setpoint: the EPO/hematocrit relationship curve shifts to the right, so that the same EPO level now maintains a higher hematocrit rather than suppressing it. This is why testosterone-induced erythrocytosis is persistent rather than transient — the body has recalibrated the target around which it regulates red cell mass. It is a concrete physiological example of the allostatic setpoint-reset principle: the regulator does not defend the old value; it defends a new, higher one.

Hepcidin Suppression

Unlocking the Iron Supply for Red Cell Production

Testosterone produces a potent, dose-dependent suppression of hepcidin — the master hormone regulating iron availability (the same hepcidin central to the iron/ferroptosis story). Hepcidin normally restrains iron release into the circulation; suppressing it increases iron availability, and since iron is the rate-limiting raw material for hemoglobin synthesis, more available iron enables more red cell production. Notably, this hepcidin suppression is stronger in older men than younger men — one reason erythrocytosis can be a more prominent issue in the over-fifty population receiving TRT. There is nuance here (animal data suggest hepcidin suppression is not strictly required for testosterone to stimulate erythropoiesis), but the increased iron availability is a well-documented parallel contributor that helps explain the magnitude of the effect.

The Real Risk: Thrombosis

Why the Hematocrit Number Matters

The concern with a rising hematocrit is increased thrombotic risk — blood clots forming where they shouldn't, potentially causing deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, stroke, or heart attack. The mechanism is viscosity-driven stasis (Virchow's triad). Important honesty about the evidence: while erythrocytosis is associated with increased thrombotic risk, no randomized or prospective study has definitively documented a direct causal link between TRT-induced erythrocytosis specifically and thromboembolic events — the risk is inferred from the strong physiological rationale and observational associations. This uncertainty does not justify complacency (the physiological case is compelling and the downside is severe), but it does mean thresholds are precautionary rather than derived from hard outcome trials. The prudent posture: treat the hematocrit as a modifiable risk marker worth controlling, while recognizing the evidence base is mechanistic more than outcome-proven.

The Amplifiers

What Makes It Worse — and What to Fix First

Several factors amplify TRT's effect on hematocrit, and identifying them is often more useful than reflexive treatment. Dose and formulation: higher doses raise hematocrit more, and injectable testosterone (especially short-acting, which produces high peaks) tends to raise it more than transdermal gels or some oral formulations — so formulation change is a legitimate lever. Untreated obstructive sleep apnea is a major independent driver — the intermittent nocturnal hypoxia stimulates EPO directly, and it stacks with the testosterone effect; screening for and treating sleep apnea can substantially help. Dehydration falsely elevates hematocrit (hemoconcentration) and should be excluded before acting on a single high value. Smoking, high altitude, and a baseline high-normal hematocrit are additional additive contributors. Addressing amplifiers is frequently the highest-yield first step.

IV. Cross-Pollination — Control Theory and the Regulated Setpoint

Erythrocytosis Is a Textbook Case of a Deliberately Shifted Control Setpoint — With All the Consequences That Implies

Testosterone-induced erythrocytosis is one of the cleanest physiological illustrations of the control-theory principles governing regulated variables. Red cell mass is a tightly regulated variable, held near a setpoint by a negative-feedback loop: low tissue oxygen raises EPO, EPO stimulates red cell production, more red cells raise oxygen delivery, and rising oxygen delivery feeds back to lower EPO — a classic homeostatic loop that defends a target hematocrit. What testosterone does is not break this loop but move its setpoint. The randomized-trial finding that testosterone shifts the EPO/hematocrit curve rightward is precisely the signature of a setpoint reset — the same phenomenon by which allostasis achieves "stability through change" by re-referencing the target rather than defending the old one. This reframing is clinically useful in three ways. First, it explains persistence: because the setpoint itself has moved, the elevated hematocrit is actively defended at the new value, which is why it does not simply drift back down on its own and why single interventions (like a one-time phlebotomy) are corrected back up by the regulator toward the new setpoint — the body refills the warehouse. Second, it clarifies why dose reduction is the most logical primary lever: lowering the testosterone dose reduces the magnitude of the setpoint shift, moving the defended target back down, rather than fighting a defended setpoint from below with repeated blood removal. Third, it illuminates the debate over phlebotomy: removing blood lowers hematocrit acutely, but if the setpoint remains shifted, the regulator senses the drop and drives production back up — and, as one 2024 hypothesis proposes, the combination of lowered tissue oxygen (from acute blood removal) and depleted iron stores (from repeated phlebotomy) could stabilize hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF) in a way that increases EPO and potentially thrombotic risk, partially offsetting the benefit of correcting the hematocrit. Whether or not that specific hypothesis holds, the control-theory lens correctly predicts that fighting a shifted setpoint from below (phlebotomy) is inherently less clean than moving the setpoint itself back down (dose reduction) — which is why guidelines position dose adjustment ahead of, or alongside, phlebotomy.

V. Antifragility & Trade-Offs
The Genuine Debate: Phlebotomy May Help, May Be Neutral, or May Partly Backfire

The management of TRT-induced erythrocytosis contains a real, unresolved controversy that a careful person should understand rather than paper over. The intuitive treatment — remove blood to lower the hematocrit (therapeutic phlebotomy or venesection) — is widely mentioned in guidelines and undeniably lowers hematocrit acutely. But its efficacy and safety for the specific purpose of reducing thrombotic risk in TRT-induced erythrocytosis are not well established by trial evidence. Three considerations complicate the intuitive picture. First, as noted, the setpoint has shifted, so phlebotomy is a repeated correction against a defended target rather than a cure — it must be maintained indefinitely. Second, repeated phlebotomy depletes iron stores, and iron-deficient erythropoiesis produces smaller, less deformable red cells that may itself affect blood rheology; it can also cause the symptoms of iron deficiency even before anemia appears. Third, the 2024 hypothesis that acute blood removal plus iron depletion could stabilize HIF and paradoxically raise EPO and thrombotic risk — while speculative — is a serious enough mechanistic concern that reflexive, aggressive phlebotomy should not be assumed to be purely beneficial. This does not mean phlebotomy is wrong; in many cases it is a reasonable and effective tool, and for a man who also has genuine iron overload it can serve double duty. It means the hierarchy matters: the least controversial and most mechanistically sound first moves are to address amplifiers (treat sleep apnea, correct dehydration, stop smoking), reduce the dose, and consider a formulation change — all of which lower the setpoint or its drivers — before or alongside resorting to repeated blood removal. The over-application error is to leave the testosterone dose untouched and simply phlebotomize on a schedule to "allow" a high dose to continue — treating the symptom (high hematocrit) while leaving the driver (excessive dose) in place, and incurring the iron-depletion and possible HIF consequences of chronic venesection. The antifragile position is to fix the driver first and use blood removal as an adjunct, not a license.

VI. The Protocol — Monitoring and Management (to Discuss With a Physician)
The Safe-TRT Monitoring and Response Framework

Baseline before starting: Hematocrit should be measured before initiating testosterone therapy. A baseline hematocrit above roughly 50% is a relative contraindication to starting — it warrants investigation (for sleep apnea, primary polycythemia, and other causes) before proceeding, because starting testosterone on an already-elevated red cell mass adds risk to risk.

Monitoring schedule: Hematocrit (as part of a complete blood count) should be checked at baseline, then typically at 3 and 6 months after starting or after any dose change, and at least annually once stable. Testosterone therapy is not a "set and forget" prescription — the hematocrit trend is the essential safety parameter, alongside testosterone levels themselves (aiming for the mid-normal range, not supraphysiologic peaks) and estradiol.

The threshold-based response (per major endocrine and urologic guidelines):
— Hematocrit within range (below ~50%): continue, routine monitoring.
— Hematocrit rising toward or above ~50–52%: investigate amplifiers first — screen for and treat obstructive sleep apnea, confirm the value isn't inflated by dehydration, address smoking; consider dose reduction or formulation change (e.g., from injectable to transdermal, or reducing injection frequency to lower peaks).
— Hematocrit above ~54%: the guideline threshold to hold testosterone until the hematocrit normalizes, then resume at a lower dose. Therapeutic phlebotomy is an option to bring it down, ideally as an adjunct to dose reduction rather than a substitute for it. Evaluate for contributing hypoxia/sleep apnea.

The mechanistically-preferred hierarchy: (1) treat amplifiers (sleep apnea especially), (2) reduce the testosterone dose to the lowest that maintains symptomatic benefit and a mid-normal testosterone level, (3) change formulation to reduce peaks, (4) use phlebotomy as an adjunct when needed, (5) discontinue if the hematocrit cannot be controlled by these means. Dose reduction is favored because it lowers the shifted setpoint at its source; phlebotomy fights the setpoint from below and must be sustained.

Hydration and testing hygiene: a single elevated hematocrit should be confirmed on a properly hydrated, standardized repeat draw before major decisions, since dehydration can transiently inflate the reading.

The one metric: hematocrit — the direct, inexpensive, and definitive parameter. Track the trend over time, not just isolated values. A hematocrit trending upward toward 50% is the early-warning signal to act on amplifiers and dose before reaching the 54% threshold. Pair it with the testosterone level (to ensure the dose is not supraphysiologic) and, where relevant, ferritin/iron studies (to monitor iron status, especially if phlebotomy is used).

VII. Recursive Synthesis

How This Connects to Hepcidin and Iron, the Control-Theory Setpoint, and the Testosterone Optimization Story

This lecture braids together several threads from the series into a single clinical knot. The iron and ferroptosis connection is direct through hepcidin: the same master iron-regulating hormone that governs the labile iron pool driving ferroptosis is dose-dependently suppressed by testosterone, linking the erythrocytosis story to iron metabolism — and creating a genuinely useful convergence, since in a man who has both a rising hematocrit and elevated iron stores, therapeutic phlebotomy addresses both simultaneously (removing red cells and the iron they contain). The control-theory connection is the conceptual spine: erythrocytosis is a textbook regulated-variable setpoint shift, and the entire management logic — favor moving the setpoint (dose) over fighting it from below (phlebotomy) — falls directly out of that framework. The testosterone optimization connection completes the picture: the goal of TRT in a man over fifty is to restore testosterone to a healthy mid-normal range for its benefits to muscle, bone, mood, libido, and metabolic health — not to push it to the top of the range or beyond, because the red-cell-stimulating effect (like most of testosterone's effects) is dose-dependent, so supraphysiologic dosing buys diminishing symptomatic returns while escalating the erythrocytosis risk. The unifying lesson is one the series returns to repeatedly: more is not better; optimal is better. The right testosterone dose is the lowest one that achieves the symptomatic and physiological benefit, because that same dose minimizes the hematocrit consequence — and monitoring the hematocrit is simply the feedback signal that tells you whether the dose has drifted past optimal into excessive.

VIII. Peer Review — Common Misconception
Corrected Misconception

Misconception: "A high hematocrit on testosterone is easily handled — just donate blood or get phlebotomized periodically, and you can keep the testosterone dose as high as you want."

Correction: This inverts the correct management hierarchy and mistakes a symptom-suppression strategy for a solution. Phlebotomy does lower hematocrit acutely, and it is a legitimate tool — but using it to enable an unnecessarily high testosterone dose is treating the consequence while feeding the cause. Three problems follow. First, because testosterone has shifted the EPO/hematocrit setpoint upward, the regulator actively drives red cell production back toward the new elevated target after each phlebotomy — so blood removal must be repeated indefinitely, a permanent treadmill rather than a fix. Second, repeated phlebotomy depletes iron stores, which brings its own consequences (iron-deficiency symptoms, altered red cell characteristics) and, per a 2024 mechanistic hypothesis, could even partly offset the intended benefit by stabilizing hypoxia-inducible factors. Third, and most fundamentally, the erythrocytosis is a dose-dependent effect: a supraphysiologic testosterone dose provides little additional symptomatic benefit over an optimal mid-normal dose while substantially increasing the hematocrit burden — so the high dose that necessitates the phlebotomy is usually not even buying meaningful additional benefit. The correct approach reverses the logic: first optimize the dose downward to the lowest level that maintains the benefit (which reduces the hematocrit at its source), treat amplifiers like sleep apnea, and use phlebotomy as a targeted adjunct when genuinely needed — not as a standing permission slip for a higher-than-necessary dose. The hematocrit is not an inconvenience to be bled away; it is the feedback signal telling you whether your testosterone dose has drifted past optimal.

Tool
The Management Hierarchy Card

In order: (1) TREAT AMPLIFIERS (sleep apnea, dehydration, smoking). (2) REDUCE DOSE to lowest effective, mid-normal T. (3) CHANGE FORMULATION to reduce peaks. (4) PHLEBOTOMY as adjunct, not substitute. (5) DISCONTINUE if uncontrollable. Favor moving the setpoint (dose) over fighting it from below (repeated blood removal).

Tool
The Optimal-Not-Maximal Rule

The target testosterone level is mid-normal — enough for symptomatic and physiological benefit, not top-of-range or supraphysiologic. Because the red-cell effect is dose-dependent, a higher dose buys little extra benefit while escalating hematocrit risk. If a dose requires routine phlebotomy to stay safe, that is usually a sign the dose is higher than it needs to be.

Secondary: the testosterone level itself (confirm mid-normal, not supraphysiologic) and, if phlebotomy is used, ferritin/iron studies to monitor iron status. Confirm any single high hematocrit on a hydrated repeat draw.
